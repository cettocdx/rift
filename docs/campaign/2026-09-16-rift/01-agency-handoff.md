# RIFT — Power, under direction

Agency handoff · 16 September 2026 · **Provisional until approved**

This is a complete campaign strategy, copy, and production specification. It is not evidence of a deployed campaign, a live product test, or approved product claims. The proposed Brand Lock is internally consistent for this draft; it becomes binding only after human approval. Existing approved logo geometry remains protected.

## What I’m assuming

- **Objective:** generate qualified requests to evaluate RIFT for a concrete security workflow. Launch stage, sales capacity, budget, and release availability have not been confirmed.
- **First audience:** hands-on security engineers and red-team operators who already use command-line tools. Security leads are the buying audience. Researchers and infrastructure teams are secondary; the launch will not try to address every technical buyer at once.
- **Offer:** a request to evaluate RIFT against an authorized workflow, subject to availability. No free trial, onboarding service, response time, or access entitlement is promised.
- **CTA:** **Request Access**, provisionally. It lets the team qualify workflow fit without implying self-service availability. The repository describes public access, so this CTA must not become an artificial gate for an already open product. If unrestricted onboarding is confirmed, change the entire campaign consistently to **Open RIFT**.
- **Scope:** a full campaign handoff: strategy, visual system, exact scripts and copy, landing-page specification, social posts, UI direction, production prompts, and review. Generated media, live ads, and a deployed page are not included in the completed outputs.
- **Evidence:** the user’s brief defines product intent. Repository documentation supplies useful qualifications, but source inspection does not establish current production behavior. No customer proof, comparative benchmark, launch price, or compliance certification has been verified.

**Campaign sentence:** For security engineers and red-team operators, this campaign makes evaluating RIFT feel like the clearest path from fragmented, manually coordinated security work to agentic execution they can direct and inspect, because it shows real tool work alongside the environment and approval choices that govern it.

**Positioning statement:** RIFT is a terminal-native agentic security workspace for operators who want to delegate complex security work while staying close to its execution. Its launch story connects the objective, the working environment, the approval decision, and the resulting tool output in one intelligible sequence.

This is a proposed positioning emphasis, not an exclusivity claim. No competitor research establishes that RIFT alone provides these capabilities. The competing habits are manual orchestration, scripts assembled for individual tasks, and separate AI advice conversations.

**Message hierarchy:**

1. Category: terminal-native agentic security workspace.
2. Value: delegate execution while retaining direction and visibility.
3. Mechanism: tools, environment selection, approval choices, and output review.
4. Evidence: a labeled, release-specific workflow recording and its limitations.
5. Action: request access with a real workflow in mind.

## Phase 3 — Three creative directions

### A. Power, under direction — recommended

- **Campaign idea:** make disciplined execution the visible expression of RIFT’s capability.
- **Emotional angle:** confidence grounded in understanding what the system is doing.
- **Hook:** “Security execution. Under your direction.”
- **Positioning angle:** a workspace for agentic action where operational decisions remain legible.
- **Visual world:** graphite planes, readable terminal output, white hierarchy, one restrained blue focus cue, a thin boundary around the active workspace. No military props or decorative targeting graphics.
- **Landing concept:** objective → environment → approval choice → execution → review. The hero shows one meaningful moment, not a collage of capabilities.
- **Ad angle:** put an action and its context in the same frame. “Before the command, know the context.”
- **Social angle:** teach operators how to evaluate autonomy through execution context, permissions, and evidence.
- **Founder/operator angle:** “Here is the question I ask before I let an agent act.” Follow with a labeled lab demonstration.
- **Product UI visualization:** one tool action, its actual environment, its actual approval state, and expandable output. Proposed elements must be labeled as concepts until present in the release.
- **Main advantage:** answers a consequential objection without making RIFT sound passive. Power and control reinforce one another.
- **Main risk:** a fabricated approval screen or an absolute safety claim would undermine the entire premise. Demonstrations must match the selected mode and release.

### B. Stay on the investigation

- **Campaign idea:** shift the operator’s attention from repeatedly coordinating tools to interpreting what the work reveals.
- **Emotional angle:** relief from fragmented attention; satisfaction in sustained technical work.
- **Hook:** “Keep your attention on the investigation.”
- **Positioning angle:** operator leverage through less manual coordination. This is a workflow hypothesis, not a measured speed promise.
- **Visual world:** tightly cropped tools and notes resolve into a single continuous working view. White reading paths on charcoal; editorial sequencing rather than HUD framing.
- **Landing concept:** follow one investigation through three handoffs: collect context, execute the next step, review the result.
- **Ad angle:** “The command is rarely the whole job.” Show the handoffs surrounding it.
- **Social angle:** breakdowns of repetitive coordination work and what operators should retain judgment over.
- **Founder/operator angle:** a narrated desk walkthrough of a fictional workflow, with the manual handoffs explicitly identified rather than exaggerated.
- **Product UI visualization:** the task transcript carries a visible relationship between objective, tool call, and result.
- **Main advantage:** immediate pain recognition; useful for operator acquisition.
- **Main risk:** easily collapses into generic productivity messaging or unsupported time savings. It gives security buyers less reason to trust execution.

### C. The next working surface

- **Campaign idea:** introduce a new place to conduct security work as agents become active participants.
- **Emotional angle:** professional ambition and a sense of a more capable operating model.
- **Hook:** “A working surface for agentic security.”
- **Positioning angle:** the agentic security workspace as a category, rather than another isolated utility.
- **Visual world:** architectural composition, stable spatial hierarchy, several work artifacts arranged around one active operation. The workspace remains the visual center.
- **Landing concept:** introduce the operator’s day through reconnaissance, validation, and follow-up, then explain the shared execution model.
- **Ad angle:** “Where should agentic security work happen?”
- **Social angle:** field notes on the relationship between operators, tools, environments, and agents.
- **Founder/operator angle:** an explicitly proposed point of view on how security work may change, paired with a present-day capability demonstration.
- **Product UI visualization:** conceptual workspace anatomy with each region labeled by purpose; no implied integrations or team features.
- **Main advantage:** creates room for a broader product story over time.
- **Main risk:** can sound like an all-encompassing security operating system before coverage is proven. Requires the most education and product breadth.

**Recommendation: A, provisional until approved.** It provides the strongest bridge between autonomous capability and a technical buyer’s need to understand execution. B is a useful secondary message, but operator efficiency is crowded territory and requires evidence. C is a later category narrative; leading with it now risks promising breadth the launch has not established.

### Reusable visual-generation specification

**Composition:** one rectangular working surface, one focal event, a calm margin for a headline. Desktop hero: 16:9 with the product on the right 58% and text space on the left. Social: recompose to 4:5; do not shrink the entire desktop layout. Vertical video: one readable detail at a time.

**Lighting:** broad soft light across graphite surfaces; subtle edge separation. The screen is readable without emitting a dramatic glow. No haze unless it remains almost imperceptible and carries no informational role.

**Lens/camera:** straight-on orthographic feel for product proof; approximately 50mm-equivalent perspective for desk scenes; controlled shallow angle only for atmospheric heroes. Essential text stays front-facing.

**Color logic:** overwhelmingly neutral; blue occupies less than approximately 5% of the composition as an art-direction target. Amber may identify a real review/wait state, never decorate the brand.

**Surface texture:** matte graphite and fine, nearly invisible grain. No scratched armor, carbon-fiber spectacle, glossy plastic dashboard, or floating glass walls.

**Typography behavior:** render typography, logo, UI, and labels in design software or code after image generation. Never trust a generated image to reproduce the product accurately. Body copy remains selectable on the landing page.

**Motion cues:** changes correspond to real events. A brief emphasis on the active item is allowed; invented progress, constant scanning lines, background cursor movement, and fake typing are not. Preserve a still version and respect reduced motion.

**Negative constraints:** no hacker hoodie, green code rain, hooded silhouette, skull, padlock collage, weapon, battlefield, random globe, server-room stock photo, unreadable code, decorative numeric data, fake brand mark, or additional product window.

**Protected details:** RIFT name; canonical logo; cybersecurity purpose; terminal-native identity; truthful distinction between local and sandbox execution; mode-specific approvals; exact product strings in any recorded view; staged labels where applicable.

**Reference roles:** canonical logo source controls identity; current app tokens control product continuity; a verified capture of the release controls UI and product language; the user’s mission-control reference controls restraint and framing only. It provides no permission to borrow another company’s marks, type layout, or endorsement. No external reference artwork was copied or commissioned.

## Phase 6 — UI and product experience direction

### What RIFT should feel like

The interface should feel ready to act and easy to question. Operators should be able to answer three questions without digging: **What is happening? Where is it happening? What needs my decision?** Cinematic depth belongs in composition, pacing, and material contrast; it must never make the operational state harder to read.

**Marketing homepage:** use a 12-column desktop grid, 1200px maximum content width, and 32–48px outer gutters. Hero copy occupies five columns, product six, with one column of separation. At narrow widths, stack copy, CTA, then product; show a meaningful crop with a way to inspect the full view. Keep section headings descriptive. The first screen should explain the category before asking the visitor to decode a visual metaphor.

**App direction:** a stable context rail, a dominant active task surface, and a contextual details panel when needed. These are layout principles, not a mandate to rebuild the current app. Preserve existing navigation unless usability evidence supports a change. Show environment and permissions near execution controls, using actual selected values. A local shell and a cloud sandbox must not share an ambiguous “secure” badge.

**Terminal-native identity:** retain prompt-to-action continuity, selectable commands, readable tool output, keyboard access, and stable scroll behavior. Use prose for intent and summaries, monospace for machine-level content. Do not style every paragraph as terminal output. TUI layouts should reflow at narrow widths rather than lose the draft or clip approval controls.

**Typography:** marketing H1 64/68px at large desktop and 40/44px on mobile, weight 500–600; H2 36/42px, mobile 28/34px; body 18/28px, mobile 16/25px. Product body approximately 13–14px with user scaling preserved; operational code 13–14px at a comfortable line height. Labels that carry meaning should remain legible at the intended export size. Use tabular numerals for aligned data. Avoid wide letter spacing in sentences.

**Spacing/grid:** use a 4px base with 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, and 96px steps. Marketing section gaps 80–120px; mobile 48–64px. Product row heights follow interaction needs, not visual density alone. Align labels, command starts, and output blocks to shared vertical edges. Use separators only where they explain grouping.

**Iconography:** one consistent thin-stroke set; no decorative reticles. Pair approval, denial, error, and environment icons with labels. Brand symbol is identity, not a substitute for every status icon. Never turn it into a spinner that visually distorts its protected geometry.

**HUD/data rules:** every indicator needs a real source, clear unit, and meaningful state. Use timestamps only when real and contextually useful. No arbitrary counters, invented confidence scores, fake coordinates, or continuously animating scanners. Avoid tiny peripheral labels that exist only to look technical.

**Code/terminal treatment:** distinguish user input, proposed action, execution output, and summary. Preserve error lines. Long commands wrap or scroll without hiding arguments in review. Copy actions must identify what they copy. Keep action detail available without flooding the main reading path. Do not turn a successful process exit into a “system secure” conclusion.

**Approval interaction design:** show the proposed action, execution target, relevant context, and available decision. Approval and denial are distinct; waiting is a real state. Do not auto-dismiss a consequential question or imply universal approvals across modes. If a mode can run without per-action prompts, disclose that at selection and near execution. Validate these patterns against the implementation before presenting them as current product behavior.

**Run states:** use the actual available state model. As a design target, distinguish proposed, awaiting decision, running, completed, failed, canceled, and uncertain. Never make a stop request visually equivalent to confirmed process termination. A disconnected view should say that status is unknown until reconciled.

**Motion:** feedback 100–160ms, panel transition 160–220ms, selected-item emphasis no more than one restrained cycle. Marketing reveals may use 250–400ms fades with 8–12px movement. These are proposed motion tokens. Avoid spring overshoot on safety-relevant controls, autoplay terminal loops, celebratory confetti, and artificial typewriter latency. Reduced-motion mode uses immediate state changes or simple fades.

**Empty states:** explain the next useful action. Suggested copy: “Choose a workflow to evaluate.” Helper: “Start with an objective and an environment you are authorized to test.” Example prompts are clearly examples, not default tasks automatically submitted. No fabricated activity history to fill space.

**Charts/metrics:** include only if they answer an operator’s question with actual data. Use zero baselines where appropriate, visible units, time ranges, and explicit no-data states. “No findings returned” must not be relabeled “No vulnerabilities.” No campaign metric widgets in the product mockup.

**Device mockups:** direct screen captures are the primary proof format. A device may establish a real working context in one supporting scene; omit manufacturer marks and exaggerated hardware hero treatment. Avoid nested device-within-browser-within-dashboard compositions.

**Consistency:** one logo source, one state vocabulary per product mode, one semantic color map, and one CTA per launch. Marketing may have more negative space than the app, but the logo, command treatment, and interface content must remain recognizable. Keep existing light themes and user appearance preferences functional; the campaign’s dark direction is not a product-wide forced theme change.

**Usability acceptance targets:** readable body text, visible focus, keyboard-operable controls, no status conveyed by color alone, usable zoom, and readable mobile crops. Verify final contrast and interaction behavior in implementation; this specification is not an accessibility certification.

### Recommended production order

1. **Approve the decision sheet:** direction A, audience priority, offer, CTA, and palette extension. Product owner confirms release surface and execution claims. No direction is deemed approved by silence.
2. **Produce the evidence asset first:** record one authorized lab workflow in the actual release. Identify environment and permission mode. Keep original footage and build identifier. Include uncertainty or failure honestly if it occurs.
3. **Finalize the hero and landing page:** use that capture, approved copy, and functional request handling. Review desktop/mobile, error and success states, links, legibility, and consent wording.
4. **Make P1 and S1:** a direct product hero and one message-led static ad establish the repeatable composition.
5. **Record C1–C3 and derive V1–V3:** use the same approved demonstration source where relevant. Creator truth and product truth receive separate review.
6. **Publish the seven-post sequence:** manually approve the real destination and proof assets before each post. This document does not schedule or publish them.
7. **Run controlled acquisition tests:** compare one message variable at a time; use qualification and evaluation progression to decide what deserves more production.

### Measurement and test plan

**Proposed funnel events:** landing_view, workflow_section_view, primary_cta_click, access_form_start, access_request_success, request_qualified, evaluation_started. Instrumentation is not implemented here. Count success only after the request is durably accepted, not on button click. Avoid putting target details or sensitive workflow descriptions in analytics payloads.

**Qualification rubric:** a relevant security role, a concrete authorized workflow, and an environment/release fit that the product team can support. A personal email is not automatically disqualifying for independent researchers.

**Metrics:** qualified requests / submitted requests; submitted requests / eligible landing sessions; evaluations started / qualified requests. For paid tests, qualified-request cost only after actual spend and qualified outcomes exist. CTR and video completion are diagnostic, not the definition of success.

**Hypotheses:**

- A control-centered hero will produce better-qualified requests than a leverage-centered hero. Keep visual, traffic source, form, and offer fixed.
- A direct product crop will improve correct product understanding relative to an atmospheric frame. Ask evaluators what the product does and where execution happens.
- A concise environment explanation near the demo will reduce mistaken assumptions about local isolation without causing an unacceptable loss of qualified intent.
- Asking for a workflow may reduce total submissions but improve evaluation fit. Compare qualification and completion together.
- A real output inspection sequence will create stronger evaluation intent than a sequence that stops after a command is launched.

Start with a baseline period and record the traffic mix. Decide the minimum meaningful change, sample requirements, and stopping rule before paid tests once baseline rates and available budget are known. Do not declare winners from a few clicks. At low volume, use moderated operator reviews and actual qualification notes. No performance outcome is guaranteed.
